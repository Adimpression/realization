# Theory of title-depth, ordered semantic determination, and decomposition guidance

## 1. Title is not external to the semantic unit

A semantic-unit title is not an arbitrary label attached from outside. It belongs to the identity of the unit itself.

This means the title does not merely help humans refer to the unit. It participates in specifying **what kind of achievedness the unit is**. The title is therefore part of the ontological constitution of the declaration, not merely a handle for retrieval or display.

From this it follows that any internal structure carried by the title is theoretically significant. If the title is flat, that flatness has meaning. If the title is segmented, that segmentation also has meaning. In either case, the title must be read as part of the semantic identity of the unit.

So the title is not secondary to decomposition. It is already one possible locus of decomposition.

---

## 2. Ordered semantic depth

When a title has internal segmentation, its parts are not to be treated as a collection of equal fragments. The deeper reading is that segmentation may express an **ordered descent of semantic determination**.

This means that earlier segments are, in principle, broader than later segments. They provide the wider semantic ground within which the later segments become intelligible. Later segments then narrow, differentiate, qualify, or actualize what is carried more broadly by the earlier ones.

So if a title has the abstract form:

`A / B / C`

it should not be read as:

`A and B and C`

nor as three neighboring labels of equal rank.

It should be read, where the title is genuinely depth-structured, as:

* `A` as broader semantic ground
* `B` as a more determinate formation within `A`
* `C` as a further determination, realization, or differentiating completion within `B` as grounded in `A`

Thus semantic segmentation is directional. It has order. It carries movement from broader to narrower, from less determined to more determined, from field to differentiation.

This is the central ontological claim.

---

## 3. Broader-before-narrower is not stylistic, but structural

The order of segments is not merely a convenient writing convention. It reflects the fact that semantic intelligibility is often layered.

A narrower determination is not self-sufficient. It is intelligible within a broader field. Therefore, when a title is segmented in a depth-revealing way, earlier segments function as conditions of intelligibility for later ones.

This gives the title an internal asymmetry:

* earlier segments can often stand as broader semantic bases
* later segments often cannot be properly understood without the earlier ones

This asymmetry is what makes title-depth theoretically meaningful. The prior items are broader categories relative to the following items. The following items are not merely subsequent words; they are determinations made possible by what comes before.

So segmentation expresses not only sequence, but dependency of meaning.

---

## 4. The title may itself already be decomposition

A semantic unit is commonly thought to be decomposed through its body, through explicit constituent structure. But a deeper theory recognizes that decomposition can begin **before** body structure appears.

A title may already carry an initial decomposition of the unit by exposing the ordered descent through which the unit becomes semantically determinate.

Therefore a declaration can contain two distinct but related forms of decomposition.

### 4.1 Title-level decomposition

The semantic unit may be internally unfolded by the ordered depth of its title. This is decomposition at the level of identity itself.

### 4.2 Body-level decomposition

The same unit may then be unfolded through explicit input and output constitution, that is, through the structure that expresses its non-achievedness and achievedness.

The first concerns the **internal semantic descent of the unit’s identity**.
The second concerns the **constituent realization structure of the unit**.

So the title is not merely what decomposition is about. It may already be the first decomposition of the thing.

This is the deep methodological point.

---

## 5. Segmentation is optional in form, but principled in theory

Not every semantic unit must expose title-depth. A unit may remain unsegmented when its identity is already semantically stable and sufficiently unified without visible internal descent.

Therefore segmentation is not a universal syntactic obligation.

But it is also not arbitrary. When present, it should be read as carrying real semantic force. A segmented title should not be treated as a cosmetic variant of an unsegmented title. It asserts that the identity of the unit contains visible ordered depth worth preserving.

So the correct theory is neither:

* every title should be segmented
* nor no title should be segmented unless absolutely necessary

The correct theory is:

**segmentation is optional at the surface, but ontologically meaningful when used.**

This means segmentation belongs to semantic theory and decomposition strategy, not to mere style.

---

## 6. Why title-depth matters for decomposition strategy

If a title expresses ordered determination, then it gives a hint about how decomposition should proceed.

A proper decomposition should not be indifferent to the semantic descent carried by the title. It should respect the broad-to-narrow structure internal to the unit.

This means:

* the earlier title segments identify the ontological field from which the unit should be decomposed
* the later title segments identify the particular differentiating or achieving determination that the decomposition must account for

Thus title-depth can guide the author by answering, before the body is even read:

* What is the broad semantic base here?
* What is being narrowed or determined?
* What kind of constitutive structure belongs naturally to this unit?
* What sort of achievedness is this declaration really about?

So title-depth is a hint on decomposition strategy because it reveals **where the decomposition should begin**, **what it should preserve**, and **what it should culminate in**.

The title, when properly formed, carries a pre-structuring of decomposition.

---

## 7. Reuse is depth-relative, not length-relative

Once title-depth is understood, the question of reuse must be reformulated.

It is not true that shorter titles are simply more reusable.
It is not true that longer titles are simply more reusable.

Those are shallow measures. The real issue is **the level at which reuse occurs**.

### 7.1 Broad-ground reuse

Earlier, broader segments are more reusable as semantic grounds. Because they are less determined, they can support many later differentiations. Their reuse lies in composition, extension, and recombination.

This is reuse as substrate.

### 7.2 Determinate-unit reuse

A fuller, deeper title is more reusable as a direct semantic unit when downstream use already requires that exact achieved determination. Its reuse lies in immediate fit and precise invocation.

This is reuse as resolved identity.

Therefore reuse is depth-relative:

* shallower units maximize reuse as grounds
* deeper units maximize reuse as determinate achieved units

The question is never bare brevity versus bare length. The question is: **at what depth does the semantic unit become the right reusable identity?**

---

## 8. Minimal sufficient determination

Because reuse is depth-relative, the title should not be optimized for minimum length or maximum depth. It should be optimized for **minimal sufficient determination**.

A semantic unit should be titled at the least depth required for it to become a stable, intelligible, reusable identity.

If the title is too shallow, then the unit remains underdetermined. It risks ambiguity, collision, and unstable reuse. The title does not yet carry enough determination to identify one coherent achieved kind.

If the title is too deep, then the unit becomes overdetermined. It starts to encode distinctions that are incidental, local, or situational. That reduces portability and makes the unit harder to reuse outside the narrow frame baked into its name.

So the right title depth is the point at which the unit first becomes stably itself.

This is the law of minimal sufficient determination:

**Title-depth should stop at the first point where the semantic unit has become a stable reusable identity, and should not continue merely for descriptive fullness.**

This is not a stylistic economy principle. It is an ontological sufficiency principle.

---

## 9. Hidden depth versus exposed depth

A semantic unit may possess internal semantic depth even when that depth is not exposed in its title.

Therefore the theory must distinguish:

* **hidden depth**, where the title remains flat though the unit has inner semantic layering
* **exposed depth**, where the title makes that layering explicit through segmentation

This distinction matters because it prevents a false conclusion that only segmented titles have ordered determination. All semantic units may have semantic depth. Segmentation is only one way of surfacing it.

So segmentation should be understood as a decision to **expose** useful depth, not to create depth ex nihilo.

This is why segmentation is a decomposition hint rather than a decomposition guarantee. It reveals a structure already judged to matter.

---

## 10. Title-depth and semantic singularity

The existence of internal title-depth does not compromise the rule that one declaration defines one semantic unit.

This is crucial.

A segmented title does not mean that the declaration contains many semantic units at the top level. Rather, it means that the one semantic unit has an internal order of determination within its identity.

So semantic singularity is preserved.

The full segmented title names one achieved kind. The segments are not separate declarations compressed into one line. They are moments of determination within one identity.

Thus the theory unites two things:

* singularity at the level of declaration
* ordered multiplicity at the level of internal determination

This is not contradiction. It is precisely how one semantic unit can be deep without becoming many units.

---

## 11. Title-depth and semantic dependency

Because earlier segments ground later ones, segmentation may be read as a statement of semantic dependency.

The dependency is not usually causal, temporal, or procedural. It is ontological and intelligibility-based.

Later segments depend on earlier ones in the sense that:

* they inherit their field of meaning from them
* they cannot be fully interpreted apart from them
* they complete a determination that is already oriented by them

So title-depth can encode a dependency path:

* not what happens first in time
* but what must be presupposed first in meaning

This makes segmentation a powerful guide not only for naming, but for theoretical analysis, schema design, validation logic, and reasoning over relations between declarations.

---

## 12. Title-depth and decomposition economy

A well-formed title can reduce decomposition confusion.

When title-depth is exposed correctly, the body no longer has to compensate for an opaque identity. The declaration begins from a more intelligible semantic position. This creates economy:

* fewer arbitrary constituents are introduced
* the decomposition is less likely to drift into unrelated context
* the distinction between ontological base and differentiating achievedness becomes clearer

Thus title-depth can serve as a discipline against decomposition sprawl.

A poor title forces the body to do too much rescue work. A good title carries part of the decomposition burden within itself.

This is why the theory belongs at the level of decomposition strategy, not mere notation.

---

## 13. Over-segmentation and under-segmentation

Because segmentation has semantic force, both excess and deficiency are theoretical errors.

### 13.1 Under-segmentation

Under-segmentation occurs when a unit’s internal broad-to-narrow structure is real and important, but the title conceals it. This can make decomposition harder, confuse reuse depth, and force the body to clarify what the title should already have made visible.

### 13.2 Over-segmentation

Over-segmentation occurs when distinctions are exposed in the title that are not ontologically primary for the identity of the unit. This reifies incidental context, bloats the identity, and narrows reuse unnecessarily.

Therefore the criterion for segmentation is not whether a unit has many words. The criterion is whether exposing internal ordered depth is semantically useful.

So the theory demands judgment: expose real depth, but only real depth.

---

## 14. Title-depth and future recomposition

The title not only hints at decomposition. It also hints at how later recomposition should occur.

A broad earlier segment may be recognized as a reusable substrate across multiple later determinations. A deeper full title may be recognized as a stable endpoint ready for direct inclusion elsewhere.

Therefore segmentation supports two future operations:

* recomposition from the level of broader grounds
* reuse from the level of fully determined units

This is why the theory of title-depth is central to scalable semantic systems. It allows the corpus to preserve both breadth and precision without collapsing them into one flat naming scheme.

---

## 15. Formal theoretical statement

The theory can be stated compactly as follows.

A semantic-unit title is part of the identity of the unit.
Where segmented, it may express an ordered descent of determination from broader semantic ground to narrower differentiation or realization.
Such segmentation is optional in surface form but meaningful in principle.
It is not merely a naming device, but a guide to decomposition strategy.

Therefore a declaration may contain two levels of decomposition:

* title-level decomposition through ordered semantic depth
* body-level decomposition through explicit constitutive structure

Reuse is depth-relative rather than length-relative:

* shallower titles are more reusable as semantic grounds
* deeper titles are more reusable as determinate achieved units

The correct degree of title-depth is given by minimal sufficient determination: the least exposed depth at which the semantic unit becomes a stable, intelligible, reusable identity.

Segmentation should therefore be used neither automatically nor sparingly as a matter of style, but only insofar as it reveals real semantic order useful for decomposition, reuse, and recomposition.

---

## 16. Strongest philosophical formulation

At the deepest level, the theory says this:

A semantic unit does not become intelligible all at once.
It may become intelligible through layers of determination.
When those layers matter, the title may reveal them.
So the title is not merely what the declaration is called.
It is the first visible descent into what the declaration is.

That is the full theory.
